Turn dense legal material into advice a non-lawyer in Egypt can understand and act on — a client update, a board summary, a risk note, or the explanation you owe a family member.

The hard part is not simplifying, it is **preserving the uncertainty while removing the jargon**. A client who understands the risk is served; a client who was reassured is not. The skill's rule is that every hedge in the analysis must survive into the explanation, and that deleting the "what is unknown" list converts advice into a promise.

Covers four failures that are specific to Egypt rather than general:

- **You are explaining across a register, not just a language.** Legal Arabic (`حيث إن`, `ومن ثَم`) exists so a court reads it unambiguously; handed to a client it produces polite incomprehension. But colloquial overshoots — for money, custody or criminal exposure it reads as unserious. The register that works is plain educated MSA. Keep the legal *terms* (`محضر`, `إنذار`, `تنفيذ`, `الشهر العقاري`) even while simplifying the grammar, because the client has to be able to say those words to a clerk.
- **Winning is not collecting.** The judgment is not money, and **execution (تنفيذ) is a separate stage** under **Law 13/1968** with its own delays. A client who wins against a party with no attachable assets has not been lied to by the court, they have been badly advised — so the skill requires the collection question to be asked *before* litigation is authorised.
- **The client's mental model came from television.** No jury, written rather than dramatic, adjournments over months. The skill's instruction is to name this concretely and to give the **sequence of steps instead of a duration**, because a made-up timeline is a promise.
- **The numbers you cannot honestly give.** How much, how long, what it costs, will I win — with the honest position on each, and a hard rule against ever stating a probability of success.

Carries the two ceilings that make clients feel cheated by their own adviser: **Civil Code Art. 227(1)**, which caps agreed interest at **7%** whatever the contract says, and **Art. 224/225**, which makes a penalty clause a ceiling rather than a floor — the client reading "10% per month" in their own contract needs to hear this *before* a budget is built on it.

Includes the rules for writing the Arabic version as its own document rather than a translation pass, and a worked unpaid-wage example showing the same claim in both a useless answer and a usable one.
