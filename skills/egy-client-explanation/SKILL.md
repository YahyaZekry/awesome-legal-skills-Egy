# Turning legal analysis into advice an Egyptian client can act on

**Not legal advice.** This skill is about *communication*, not about what the law
is. Everything it tells you to say about substance must come from a skill that has
verified that substance — never from this one.

## What this skill does

It converts material written for lawyers into material a client can use. A
pleading, an internal memo, counsel's note, a contract, a judgment — in, and out
comes something a person with no legal training can act on today.

The hard part is not simplifying. Simplifying is easy and mostly harmful. The hard
part is **preserving the uncertainty** while removing the jargon — a client who
understands the risk is served; a client who was reassured is not.

## The four things that go wrong in Egypt specifically

Generic "explain it plainly" advice will not prepare you for these.

### 1. You are explaining across a **register**, not just a language

If the analysis is in English and the client reads Arabic, translating is the easy
half. ⚠️ The harder half is that **Egyptian legal Arabic and the Arabic a client
reads are not the same language in practice.**

Legal Arabic is dense, nominal and full of fossilised formulae — `حيث إن`, `ومن
ثَم`, `بموجب`, `المُدَّعى عليه`. It exists so that a court reads it unambiguously.
A client who is handed it will tell you they understand and then act as though they
did not.

**Do not overshoot either.** Writing the explanation in Egyptian colloquial
(`عامية`) sounds friendly and reads as unserious when the topic is money, custody
or criminal exposure. The register that works is **plain, educated Modern Standard
Arabic** — short sentences, active voice, common words, no formulae.

> **The test:** read it aloud. If it sounds like a news bulletin, it is right. If it
> sounds like a court filing, it is wrong. If it sounds like a text message, it is
> also wrong.

Keep the Egyptian legal **terms** even when you simplify the sentence — `محضر`,
`إنذار`, `الشهر العقاري`, `تنفيذ`, `نيابة`. A client has to be able to say these
words to a clerk or a police officer. Replacing them with a generic Arabic
equivalent is a disservice, because the document they will be handed is titled with
the term you removed.

### 2. Winning is not collecting — ✅ the single most important correction

This is the misconception that does the most damage, and it is not primarily a
language problem.

A judgment is not money. In Egypt, **execution (تنفيذ) is a separate stage with its
own court, its own procedure and its own delays** — see
`skills/egy-court-filing-guide/` and `research/egyptian-corpus/03-employment-courts-aml-banking.md`
(**Law 13/1968** governs execution). A client who wins and then discovers that the
other side has no attachable assets has been badly advised, and will experience it
as having been lied to.

**So: always state the collection question explicitly.** Before a client authorises
spending on litigation, they should know:

- Does the other side have attachable assets you can name?
- Is there a bank account, a salary, a vehicle, a registered property?
- If not, what does winning actually get them?

⚠️ Be careful with the reverse error too. Do not talk a client out of a claim that
is worth bringing — a judgment is still worth having against a party who may
acquire assets, and it interrupts prescription. Say what is true: the judgment
secures the **right**, and collecting depends on finding **assets**.

### 3. The client's mental model came from somewhere else

Most people's picture of how legal disputes work comes from American and British
television: a trial, a jury, a dramatic cross-examination, a verdict within the
hour, damages announced as a number.

Egyptian civil and commercial litigation has **no jury**, is **written rather than
dramatic**, and proceeds through adjournments (`جلسات`) spread over months or
years. The client who expects a television trial will read every adjournment as a
catastrophe or as evidence that you are failing.

**Name this early and concretely.** Not "litigation takes time" — that is
invisible. Say what actually happens next, and what has to happen after that.

⚠️ **Do not invent timelines.** Court delays vary by circuit, by case type and by
year, and this project has deliberately not verified figures for them. If you are
asked "how long", give the sequence of steps and say plainly that you will not
guess the duration. A made-up timeline is a promise.

### 4. The numbers you cannot honestly give

Clients ask for four numbers, and in Egypt at least two of them are usually
unknowable in advance:

| The client asks | The honest position |
|---|---|
| **"How much will I get?"** | Explain the measure, not a figure — and see the penalty-clause ceiling below |
| **"How long will it take?"** | Give the sequence. ⚠️ Do not give dates |
| **"What will it cost?"** | Judicial fees are computable from the claim value; ⚠️ the 2024–25 Court of Appeal tariffs are **administrative, not legislative**, and are under challenge — see `egy-court-filing-guide` |
| **"Will I win?"** | Give the strength of the position and the main risk. Never a probability |

⚠️ **Two ceilings silently reduce what a client thinks they are owed**, and getting
this wrong is how a client ends up feeling cheated by their own adviser:

- **Civil Code Art. 227(1)** — agreed interest is capped at **7%**. A contract
  promising more is not enforceable at the higher rate.
- **Civil Code Art. 224/225** — a penalty clause is a **ceiling, not a floor**. The
  client who reads "10% per month late fee" in their own contract believes they are
  owed 10% per month. They are not. Say so **before** they build a budget on it.

✅ **One thing that genuinely helps, and is often missed:** worker claims are
**exempt from judicial fees at every stage** under **Labour Law 14/2025 Art. 7**,
and are enforceable immediately without bail. For an employee weighing up whether
to pursue unpaid wages, that can be decisive — see `egy-employment-dispute`.

## Output structure

Eight parts. Cut nothing; cut the *length* of each instead.

### 1. The answer in one line

The question they asked, answered, in one sentence. If the honest answer is
"it depends", the one line is *what it depends on*.

### 2. What this means for you

Consequences, not law. Not "Art. 227(1) caps interest" but "you will not get the
10% a month the contract says".

### 3. Certain, likely, unknown

Three explicit lists. **The `unknown` list is the most valuable part of the
document** and the one everyone deletes. Deleting it converts your advice into a
guarantee.

### 4. Your options, with the real trade-off

Two or three, each with what it costs and what it forecloses. An option presented
without its downside is not an option, it is a recommendation wearing a disguise.

### 5. Cost and time, honestly

See §4 above. Sequence and certainty, not figures and dates.

### 6. What to do next

Numbered, dated where a deadline is real, and only things **they** must do.
Distinguish clearly from what you will do.

### 7. The questions you will be asked

The five hardest things this client will ask, with your answers. This is the part
that saves the phone call — and writing it exposes the parts of your own analysis
that are still soft.

### 8. The Arabic version

Not a footnote. See below.

## Writing the Arabic version

If the client reads Arabic, ⚠️ **the Arabic version is the real one** — the English
is your working copy. Treating Arabic as a translation step is how the substance
gets quietly lost.

- **Answer first, in the first line.** A reader who stops after one line should
  still have the answer.
- **One idea per sentence.** Legal Arabic packs three into one; you are undoing
  that.
- **Use tables for comparisons**, prose for consequences. Egyptian readers of
  business Arabic read tables well.
- **Keep the legal terms**, simplify the grammar — see §1.
- **Mark uncertainty in the text, not in a footnote.** `لم يُتحقَّق منه` next to the
  thing that is unverified.
- **Repeat the number and the date**, do not use `المذكور أعلاه`. People read
  Arabic advice in fragments, on a phone, out of order.

⚠️ **Arabic output in this project has corrupted five separate times** — stray
Cyrillic, CJK and Portuguese fragments, repeated digit runs, duplicated words,
half-finished sentences. Blockquote and template regions are the highest risk.
**Read the Arabic back against the English before sending it.** Run
`python3 .project-knowledge/scripts/validate.py` if you are writing into this repo.

## Worked example — an unpaid-wage claim

**Client's actual question:** "Can I sue them? I'm owed four months."

**A bad answer** (all true, all useless): *"Under Labour Law 14/2025 and the
limitation rules in Civil Code Art. 378, together with Art. 378(2), your claim for
wages is subject to a one-year limitation and requires an oath where documentation
is incomplete; jurisdiction lies with the specialised labour court, and judicial
fees are waived at all stages pursuant to Art. 7."*

**The Egyptian-edition answer:**

> **You can claim, and you are in a stronger position than most — but the amount
> you recover depends on where they bank, and there is a one-year clock.**
>
> **What this means for you:** the four months of wages are claimable, and unlike
> most claims you do not pay court fees to bring it — worker claims are exempt at
> every stage (Labour Law 14/2025 Art. 7), and the judgment can be enforced
> immediately without posting bail. That matters, because it means the usual cost
> barrier does not apply to you.
>
> **What is certain:** you are owed wages for time worked. You do not pay fees.
> **What is likely:** interest on the arrears, capped at 7% — **not** whatever the
> contract says, if it says more (Civil Code Art. 227(1)).
> **What is unknown:** whether they have attachable assets, and therefore whether
> winning produces money.
>
> **Your options:** (1) **إنذار first** — a formal demand served by bailiff, cheap,
> and it interrupts prescription. Downside: it warns them, and they may move
> assets. (2) **File directly** — faster to a judgment, and you are fee-exempt.
> Downside: if they have nothing to attach, you hold a judgment and no money.
>
> **Cost and time:** no fees. I am not going to give you a duration — I will tell
> you the steps instead: file → first session → adjournments → judgment → تنفيذ.
>
> **⚠️ The deadline that decides this:** wage claims carry a **one-year
> limitation** (Civil Code Art. 378). If you are near the year, this is urgent, and
> it is the reason to act before deciding anything else.
>
> **What to do next:** (1) Do not sign anything from them. (2) Gather your payslips
> and the contract this week. (3) Tell me whether you know their bank.
>
> **Questions you will ask:** *"Will I get the money?"* → If they bank somewhere we
> can find, yes. If they have nothing, the judgment preserves your right and I will
> not pretend otherwise. *"Can they fire me for this?"* → That is a separate
> question with its own protections, and I will answer it properly rather than
> guess here.

## What never to do

- ❌ **Never give a probability of success.** "Strong case" is a judgement;
  "80%" is a fabrication.
- ❌ **Never let the plain version become more certain than the analysis.** Every
  hedge in your analysis must survive into the explanation. This is the single
  most common failure and it is invisible to the person doing it.
- ❌ **Never translate an English legal concept into an Egyptian word that does not
  mean it.** `disclosure` is not `إفصاح` in the Egyptian litigation sense; there is
  no Egyptian equivalent procedure. Say what actually happens instead.
- ❌ **Never reassure to end an uncomfortable conversation.** The client's comfort
  is not the deliverable.
- ❌ **Never present the Arabic as "a translation".** See §8.

## Workflow

1. **Identify the client's actual question** — usually narrower than the analysis.
2. **Extract the substance from a skill that verified it.** Do not restate law from
   this skill.
3. **Write the one-liner first.** If you cannot, you do not yet understand your own
   analysis.
4. **Build the three lists** — certain / likely / unknown.
5. **Answer the collection question explicitly.**
6. **Strip the register**, keep the terms. Read it aloud — §1.
7. **Write the Arabic as its own document**, not as a translation pass.
8. **Send both**, and say which one is authoritative.
